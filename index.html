Understanding how government shutdowns work and their impact

A government shutdown occurs when Congress fails to pass or the President refuses to sign a spending bill to fund federal government operations. During a shutdown, non-essential federal agencies cease operations until funding legislation is passed and signed into law.

Government shutdowns primarily affect federal employees, contractors, and individuals who rely on federal services. Essential employees continue working but may not receive pay until the shutdown ends. Non-essential employees are furloughed.

Essential employees required to work during a shutdown do not receive paychecks during the shutdown but are guaranteed back pay once it ends. Since the passage of the Government Employee Fair Treatment Act of 2019 (GEFTA), furloughed employees are also guaranteed back pay. However, federal contractors typically do not receive back pay.

The duration varies significantly. Most shutdowns last just a few days, but some have extended much longer. The longest shutdown in U.S. history was from December 2018 to January 2019, lasting 35 days.

Shutdowns occur due to political disagreements over federal spending and policy priorities. Common causes include disputes over the federal budget, the debt ceiling, funding for specific programs or policies (like healthcare, immigration, or infrastructure), and partisan disagreements between the executive and legislative branches.

A shutdown ends when Congress passes and the President signs either: (1) appropriations bills funding the affected agencies for the full fiscal year, (2) a continuing resolution that provides temporary funding at previous levels, or (3) a compromise agreement that addresses the disputed issues. Sometimes partial funding bills are passed to reopen some agencies while negotiations continue on others.
